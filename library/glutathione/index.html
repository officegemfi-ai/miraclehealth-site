What it is

Glutathione is a small peptide the body makes and keeps in blood and tissues, where it acts as an antioxidant that helps limit oxidative stress. It is sold as an oral supplement, including liposomal forms, and some clinics give it by IV infusion or injection. In a 6-month trial, taking it by mouth raised the body's glutathione stores, but whether this improves health has not been shown.

Limited evidence

One good randomized trial showed oral glutathione raises the body's stores; evidence for liver disease and skin lightening comes from small or uncontrolled studies.

What it may do

  • Moderate evidence

    Raised glutathione in red cells, plasma and immune cells (lymphocytes) by about 30% to 35% over 6 months in the higher-dose group of a placebo-controlled trial of 54 healthy adults taking it by mouth; levels returned to baseline a month after stopping 4

  • Limited evidence

    Higher natural killer (immune) cell activity at 3 months in the same trial's higher-dose group 4

  • Limited evidence

    Lower ALT (a liver enzyme), triglycerides and ferritin after 4 months in an uncontrolled study of 29 people with fatty liver disease 5

  • Limited evidence

    Some skin lightening from oral and topical forms in small trials, with inconsistent results; effects of IV use appeared to fade over about 6 months 6

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    Contaminated injections: by August 2026 FDA knew of at least 30 people with fever, chills, dizziness and shock- or sepsis-like reactions after IV glutathione made from supplement-grade ingredient, some needing hospital care 3

  • Not known

    Liver injury and severe allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, reported with IV use for skin lightening 6

  • Not known

    Mild, short-lived stomach upset such as gas or loose stools with oral forms 6

  • Not known

    In the fatty liver study, liver enzymes rose in people who did not respond and HbA1c (a blood sugar marker) rose overall; three people dropped out with fatigue, high blood pressure or rash (cause unclear) 5

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (not studied)
  • Liver disease, unless supervised by a clinician (liver injury has been reported with IV use)
  • People considering IV use outside a closely supervised clinical setting

Interactions

  • None well documented

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

  • Liver enzymes (ALT, AST) if used for liver disease

While taking it

  • Liver enzymes (ALT, AST) if used for liver disease

No routine monitoring is needed for oral supplements; the fatty liver study tracked ALT and AST, which rose in some patients.

Key studies

  1. RCT201554 healthy non-smoking adults (6 months)

    Randomized controlled trial of oral glutathione supplementation on body stores of glutathione

    Oral glutathione raised glutathione in blood, red cells, plasma, immune cells and cheek cells, depending on dose and time, and lowered a marker of oxidative stress; levels returned to baseline after a month off. 4

  2. Trial201729 people with fatty liver disease (34 enrolled), no control group

    Efficacy of glutathione for the treatment of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease: an open-label, single-arm, multicenter, pilot study

    After 4 months of oral glutathione following lifestyle advice, ALT, triglycerides and ferritin fell, and liver fat fell in responders only; the authors call the results preliminary. 5

  3. Review2025Studies of oral, topical and IV glutathione

    Exploring the Safety and Efficacy of Glutathione Supplementation for Skin Lightening: A Narrative Review

    Oral and topical glutathione may lighten skin, with inconsistent results and mild side effects; IV use raises serious safety concerns and its effect appears short-lived. 6

Questions for your clinician

  • Is there a specific reason, such as a liver condition, that glutathione might help me?
  • Is there an approved product, or evidence that IV glutathione is safe for my situation?
  • Should my liver enzymes be checked before and during use?

Status in the US

Compounded by prescription Compoundable while FDA evaluates; also a supplement. FDA lists glutathione in Category 1 (under evaluation), so pharmacies may compound it, including injections, while FDA decides; oral products are sold as supplements. In August 2026 FDA warned compounders not to make injections from supplement-grade glutathione after at least 30 patients had reactions. 123
