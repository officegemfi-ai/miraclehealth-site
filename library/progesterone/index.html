What it is

Progesterone is a female sex hormone made by the ovaries. In women taking estrogen after menopause, it counters estrogen's growth effect on the lining of the uterus. Prometrium is micronized progesterone, made from a plant-derived starting material and chemically identical to the hormone the ovaries make. It differs from synthetic progestins such as medroxyprogesterone acetate, the progestin used in the WHI trial.

Strong evidence

FDA-approved to protect the uterine lining during estrogen therapy and to treat secondary amenorrhea, based on randomized trials; evidence for hot flashes and sleep with progesterone alone is limited and mixed.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Protects the uterine lining in postmenopausal women taking estrogen: overgrowth (hyperplasia) in 6% versus 64% with estrogen alone over up to 3 years (358 women) 1

  • Strong evidence

    Brings on a period in women whose periods have stopped: 80% had withdrawal bleeding versus 10% with placebo 1

  • Moderate evidence

    Fewer and milder hot flashes and night sweats when taken alone by 133 women in early postmenopause over 12 weeks (off-label) 5

  • Limited evidence

    Better self-rated sleep quality and night sweats in 189 perimenopausal women over 3 months, although the main hot-flash score did not differ from placebo (off-label) 6

Risks and side effects

  • Common

    Breast tenderness (27% versus 6%), bloating, dizziness, headache and low mood in a 3-year trial taken with estrogen 1

  • Common

    Drowsiness and dizziness, which can affect driving 1

  • Rare

    With estrogen plus a synthetic progestin, WHI found about 9 extra strokes, 9 extra lung clots and 12 extra leg clots per 10,000 women per year; the label carries these warnings although WHI did not test progesterone itself 1

  • Not known

    Breast cancer: estrogen plus micronized progesterone did not raise risk for up to 5 years, with limited evidence of higher risk after longer use 7

  • Not known

    Compounded 'bioidentical' hormone products: risk of getting too much or too little, impurities, and no product label describing risks 4

Who should skip it, or ask a doctor first

  • Peanut allergy (Prometrium capsules contain peanut oil)
  • Unexplained vaginal bleeding
  • Current or past breast cancer
  • Current or past blood clots, stroke or heart attack
  • Liver disease

What it can clash with

  • Ketoconazole and other drugs that block the liver enzyme CYP3A4 may raise progesterone levels (clinical importance unknown)

Blood tests to watch

Before starting

  • Breast exam and mammogram as recommended for age
  • Pelvic exam
  • Evaluation of any unexplained vaginal bleeding

While taking it

  • Yearly breast exam, pelvic exam and mammogram
  • Prompt evaluation of any unusual vaginal bleeding

The label asks for yearly pelvic and breast exams with mammography and prompt evaluation of unusual bleeding; it does not call for routine blood tests.

The main studies

  1. Systematic review2018Studies of postmenopausal women using estrogen with micronized progesterone

    The impact of micronized progesterone on breast cancer risk: a systematic review

    Up to 5 years of estrogen with oral or vaginal micronized progesterone did not raise breast cancer risk; there was limited evidence of higher risk with longer use. 7

  2. RCT2012133 healthy women 1 to 10 years after menopause

    Oral micronized progesterone for vasomotor symptoms--a placebo-controlled randomized trial in healthy postmenopausal women

    Over 12 weeks, the combined hot flash and night sweat score fell by 10.0 points with progesterone versus 4.4 with placebo. 5

  3. RCT2023189 perimenopausal women

    Oral micronized progesterone for perimenopausal night sweats and hot flushes: a Phase III Canada-wide randomized placebo-controlled 4-month trial

    After 3 months the main hot-flash score did not differ significantly from placebo, but women rated night sweats, sleep quality and interference with daily life as better on progesterone. 6

  4. Label2026358 postmenopausal women with a uterus taking estrogen

    PROMETRIUM (progesterone, USP) capsules: Prescribing Information

    Endometrial hyperplasia developed in 6% with added progesterone versus 64% with estrogen alone over up to 36 months. 1

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Do I need progesterone to protect my uterus with the estrogen I take?
  • Could drowsiness or dizziness affect my driving or interact with my other medicines?
  • Would an FDA-approved product be safer for me than a compounded cream?

Where it stands in the US

FDA-approved FDA-approved prescription hormone (Prometrium). Approved to prevent overgrowth of the uterine lining (endometrial hyperplasia) in postmenopausal women with a uterus taking estrogen, and to treat periods that have stopped (secondary amenorrhea). FDA lists Prometrium among menopausal hormone products relabeled in February 2026, when heart disease, breast cancer and dementia statements were removed from boxed warnings; the current label shows no boxed warning, and the Menopause Society has raised safety concerns about compounded 'bioidentical' hormones. 1234
