Also called N-acetyl cysteine, N-acetyl-L-cysteine, NAC

What it is

N-acetylcysteine (acetylcysteine) is the N-acetyl form of the amino acid L-cysteine. After an acetaminophen overdose it probably protects the liver by maintaining or restoring glutathione levels and by helping neutralize a toxic breakdown product of acetaminophen; inhaled, it thins abnormally thick mucus. It was approved as a drug in 1963, and products labeled as dietary supplements have been sold in the US for over 30 years.

Limited evidence

It is an approved antidote for acetaminophen overdose, but evidence for everyday supplement uses is weak or conflicting; a 2023 meta-analysis of 9 COPD trials found no benefit.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Approved antidote that prevents or lessens liver injury after a potentially toxic acetaminophen overdose, given by mouth or IV in emergency care, where it works best the sooner it is started; supplement NAC taken at home is no substitute 34

  • Strong evidence

    Approved as an inhaled add-on treatment to thin abnormally thick mucus in lung conditions such as chronic bronchopulmonary disease and cystic fibrosis 4

  • Limited evidence

    Fewer COPD flare-ups in six meta-analyses published before 2020, mostly of trials from before 2000; a 2023 meta-analysis of 9 trials since 2000 (2,137 adults) found no fewer flare-ups and no slower lung-function decline 5

Risks and side effects

  • Common

    Nausea, vomiting and other stomach symptoms, common with oral use, especially at high doses 4

  • Common

    Allergic-type (hypersensitivity) reactions to the IV antidote, mostly rash, flushing and itching: 17% of patients in the label's infusion study and 8% of adults and 10% of children in a large safety study; anaphylaxis, which can be life-threatening, in about 1 to 2 per 1,000 3

  • Not known

    Airway narrowing (bronchospasm), which can occur unpredictably with the inhaled form; people with asthma need close watching 43

  • Not known

    Low blood pressure with nitroglycerin: in a hospital trial in unstable angina, symptomatic low blood pressure occurred in 7 patients given nitroglycerin plus IV NAC versus none given nitroglycerin alone 6

  • Rare

    Rash, with or without mild fever, and hives, rarely with oral use 4

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Possible acetaminophen (Tylenol) overdose: get emergency care rather than self-treating
  • Asthma (clinician first)
  • Taking nitroglycerin or another nitrate for the heart (clinician first)
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (clinician first; not well studied in pregnancy)

Interactions

  • Nitroglycerin: NAC can strengthen its blood-pressure-lowering effect; symptomatic low blood pressure was more common with the combination in a hospital trial
  • Activated charcoal: binds acetylcysteine in lab tests and may reduce its effect, per the drug label

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

None routinely needed.

While taking it

None routinely needed.

No routine tests are needed for supplement use; when it is given as an antidote, blood acetaminophen levels guide treatment.

Key studies

  1. Meta-analysis20232,137 adults with COPD in 9 randomized trials published from 2000 to 2022

    The efficacy of N-acetylcysteine in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease patients: a meta-analysis

    Long-term oral NAC did not reduce flare-ups, slow lung-function decline or improve quality of life versus placebo, with similar side effects; 4 of the 9 trials had a high risk of bias. 5

  2. Label2025Safety studies in 4,709 adults and 1,905 children treated for acetaminophen overdose

    ACETADOTE (acetylcysteine) injection, for intravenous use: prescribing information (revised July 2025)

    Hypersensitivity reactions occurred in 8% of adults and 10% of children, mostly rash, flushing and itching; anaphylaxis occurred in 0.1% of adults and 0.2% of children. 3

  3. Trial1988Patients hospitalized with unstable angina

    Combined use of nitroglycerin and N-acetylcysteine in the management of unstable angina pectoris

    Symptomatic low blood pressure occurred in 7 patients given nitroglycerin with IV NAC versus none given nitroglycerin alone; a later group given NAC by continuous infusion had none. 6

Questions for your clinician

  • Could NAC interact with my medicines or affect my asthma?
  • Is there evidence it would help my specific liver or lung condition, and how would we tell?
  • What should I do if I ever think I have taken too much acetaminophen?

Status in the US

Restricted Approved drug; supplement sales tolerated by FDA. Approved since 1963; current labels cover acetaminophen overdose (by mouth or IV) and, inhaled, thick mucus in lung disease. Because it was a drug first, FDA says NAC is excluded from the definition of a dietary supplement, but since August 2022 it has said it will not act against otherwise lawful NAC supplements while it considers rulemaking; no final decision was found as of October 2026. 1234
