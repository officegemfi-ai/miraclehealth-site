What it is

Crystagen is a lab-made chain of three amino acids, glutamic acid, aspartic acid and proline (Glu-Asp-Pro, or EDP), from Vladimir Khavinson's peptide research in Russia. Its developers patented it as an 'immunogeroprotector', meaning a substance meant to slow the age-related decline of the immune system. In their lab and rat studies it boosted the growth and maturation of immune cells and helped protect the thymus, the gland where T cells mature.

Limited evidence

Rat and cell studies plus small studies in older adults with no randomization described, all reported by the developers in a patent and a review.

What it may do

  • Limited evidence

    Immune blood tests returned to normal in more older adults with weakened immunity when it was added to standard care (82% vs 56%); reported by the developers, and not described as randomized 45

  • Limited evidence

    Less fatigue and weakness reported by older adults taking it 4

  • Animal and lab studies

    Less shrinking of the thymus, with its structure better preserved, in rats after radiation 45

  • Animal and lab studies

    More growth of thymus and spleen tissue from old rats, and more immune-signaling activity by cells from aging mice, in lab culture 5

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    No independent human safety data; reports of no side effects come from the developers 45

  • In theory

    Because it is meant to stimulate immune cells, it could in theory worsen autoimmune disease or work against anti-rejection drugs 5

  • Not known

    No FDA-approved or pharmacy-compounded version exists, so the identity and purity of products are unverified 13

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Autoimmune disease, an organ transplant, or taking immune-suppressing medicines, unless a clinician is involved
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (never studied)
  • Under 18 (never studied)

Interactions

  • Immune-suppressing medicines: could in theory work against each other (not studied)

Blood markers to watch

Not an approved medicine. These are checks a clinician or a clinical trial would use, not a plan for taking it on your own.

Before starting

  • General health check
  • Complete blood count with a white-cell breakdown

While taking it

None routinely needed.

No evidence-based monitoring exists for Crystagen; the developers judged its effects with immune blood tests, which a clinician could repeat if there is a reason to.

Key studies

  1. Trial201170 older adults with weakened immunity after environmental radiation exposure: 38 treated, 32 on standard care

    Peptide substance revealing an immunogeroprotective effect, pharmaceutical composition on its base and the method of its application

    Immune measures returned to normal in 82% of treated people versus 56% of controls, with no side effects reported. The patent does not describe randomization or blinding. 5

  2. Review2021Older adults, rats and cell cultures (the developers' studies)

    The Use of Thymalin for Immunocorrection and Molecular Aspects of Biological Activity

    Summarizes the developers' work: the tripeptide EDP (crystagen) improved immune test results and fatigue in older adults, protected the thymus in irradiated rats, and slowed the growth of a leukemia cell line in culture. 4

Questions for your clinician

  • Do I actually have weakened immunity, and what is causing it?
  • Could this affect any immune condition I have or medicine I take?
  • Which vaccines or proven steps would protect me more?

Status in the US

Not approved Not FDA-approved; not on FDA compounding lists. Crystagen is not an FDA-approved drug and appears on none of FDA's compounding lists (nominated substances, updated May 2026; safety-risk and withdrawn nominations, April 2026). FDA allows pharmacy compounding only from ingredients that are in an approved drug, meet a USP standard, or are on or under review for its bulk list. 123
