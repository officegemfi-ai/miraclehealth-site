What it is

MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded by a short stretch of mitochondrial DNA (inside the 12S rRNA gene), and it circulates naturally in the blood of people and rodents. In mice and rats, giving extra MOTS-c changed how the body handles glucose and fat. It is not an approved drug, and FDA found no studies in which it was given to people.

Animal and lab studies

All evidence of benefit comes from mouse, rat and cell studies; FDA found no clinical studies of MOTS-c given to people and no toxicity studies.

What it may do

  • Animal and lab studies

    Less weight gain and lower insulin levels in mice fed a high-fat diet 3

  • Animal and lab studies

    Lower blood glucose in obese mice, and protection from obesity and insulin resistance in female mice after ovary removal 3

  • Animal and lab studies

    Better physical capacity and healthspan in aged mice 3

  • Animal and lab studies

    Less bone loss in mice and less blood-vessel calcification in rats 3

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    Human safety is unknown: FDA found no clinical studies and no animal toxicity studies to show what it does to people 3

  • In theory

    Immune reactions when injected, from the peptide itself or from impurities and clumped (aggregated) peptide 3

  • In theory

    Effects on other organs are hard to predict, according to FDA 3

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (no safety data)
  • Under 18 (no safety data)
  • Diabetes or taking glucose-lowering medicine, unless a clinician is involved (it lowered blood sugar in mice)

Interactions

  • Diabetes medicines: theoretical only, because MOTS-c lowered blood glucose in obese mice; not studied in people

Blood markers to watch

Not an approved medicine. These are checks a clinician or a clinical trial would use, not a plan for taking it on your own.

Before starting

  • General health check, including fasting glucose or HbA1c

While taking it

  • General health check

No evidence-based monitoring exists for MOTS-c; blood sugar is the most relevant marker only because animal studies show effects on glucose.

Key studies

  1. Review2026FDA review of rodent and cell studies

    FDA Briefing Document for MOTS-c-Related Bulk Drug Substances (Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee, July 23-24, 2026)

    FDA found no studies of MOTS-c given to people and no toxicity studies, raised immunogenicity concerns, and proposed not adding it to the compounding list. 3

  2. Animal2015Mice on a high-fat diet

    The Mitochondrial-Derived Peptide MOTS-c Promotes Metabolic Homeostasis and Reduces Obesity and Insulin Resistance

    MOTS-c reduced diet-induced weight gain and high insulin levels in mice. 3

  3. Animal2021Aged mice

    MOTS-c Is an Exercise-Induced Mitochondrial-Encoded Regulator of Age-Dependent Physical Decline and Muscle Homeostasis

    Treatment improved physical capacity in older mice, and FDA's summary notes that lifespan also increased. 3

Questions for your clinician

  • Since MOTS-c has never been tested in people, what would make it worth the risk for me?
  • Would exercise or an approved medicine do more for my blood sugar or weight goals?
  • If I have diabetes, how could this affect my blood sugar and medicines?

Status in the US

Under FDA review Not approved; compounding status under FDA review. FDA dropped MOTS-c from its Category 2 safety-risk list in April 2026 after the nominator withdrew it. FDA still reviewed it: staff proposed not adding it to the list of substances pharmacies may compound, and its advisory committee voted 7 to 5 (2 abstentions) for adding it in July 2026. As of mid-October 2026 no rule or interim policy allowing compounding had been published. 12345
