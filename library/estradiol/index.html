What it is

Estradiol (17-beta-estradiol) is the main estrogen made by the ovaries before menopause and the most potent of the body's estrogens. Prescription estradiol is the same molecule and comes in several forms, including tablets, skin gels and patches, and vaginal products. It works through estrogen receptors in tissues such as the uterus, breast and bone, and after menopause it lowers the high LH and FSH levels.

Strong evidence

Many randomized trials, pooled in a Cochrane review, support hormone therapy for hot flashes, and estradiol is FDA-approved for menopausal symptoms, vaginal atrophy, low-estrogen states and osteoporosis prevention.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Cuts hot flash frequency by about 75% versus placebo and eases night sweats in menopausal women 54

  • Strong evidence

    Treats vaginal dryness and thinning (vulvar and vaginal atrophy) after menopause 1

  • Strong evidence

    Approved to prevent postmenopausal osteoporosis in women at significant risk who cannot use non-estrogen drugs; in the WHI trials, hormone therapy (conjugated estrogens, not estradiol) cut hip fractures by a third 16

  • Strong evidence

    Replaces estrogen in women with low estrogen from hypogonadism, removal of the ovaries or primary ovarian failure 1

  • Moderate evidence

    Slower thickening of the neck artery wall when oral estradiol was started within 6 years of menopause, but not when started 10 or more years after 7

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    Endometrial cancer if estrogen is taken without a progestogen by a woman with a uterus: risk reported 2 to 12 times higher than in non-users (boxed warning) 41

  • Rare

    Blood clots and stroke: about 9 extra strokes and 9 extra lung clots per 10,000 women per year on combined therapy in WHI; in a large UK study, tablets raised clot risk but patches and gels did not 68

  • Uncommon

    Breast cancer: risk rises with longer use and more with estrogen plus progestogen; about 1 extra case per 200 women using estrogen alone for 5 years from age 50, and 1 per 50 with a daily progestogen 69

  • Common

    Breast tenderness, irregular bleeding or spotting, headache, bloating and nausea 14

  • Not known

    Gallbladder disease needing surgery, reported 2 to 4 times more often in postmenopausal estrogen users 16

  • Not known

    Probable dementia was about doubled when combined therapy was started at 65 or older in WHI; FDA removed this warning from labels in 2025-2026 62

Talk to a clinician first if you are

  • Current or past breast cancer, or another estrogen-sensitive cancer
  • Unexplained vaginal bleeding
  • Current or past blood clots, stroke or heart attack, or a known clotting disorder
  • Liver disease
  • Pregnancy

Interactions

  • St. John's wort, carbamazepine, phenobarbital and rifampin can lower estrogen levels and its effect
  • Clarithromycin, erythromycin, ketoconazole, itraconazole, ritonavir and grapefruit juice can raise estrogen levels and side effects
  • Thyroid hormone: estrogen raises thyroid-binding protein, so people on thyroid replacement may need their dose rechecked

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

  • Breast exam and mammogram as recommended for age
  • Blood pressure
  • Triglycerides if they are known to be high
  • Evaluation of any unexplained vaginal bleeding

While taking it

  • Breast exams and mammography as recommended
  • Blood pressure at regular intervals
  • Thyroid function in people on thyroid hormone
  • Endometrial sampling if unexplained bleeding occurs

Labels advise breast screening, blood pressure checks and prompt review of abnormal bleeding; blood FSH and estradiol levels are not useful for managing menopausal symptoms, which are judged by how symptoms respond.

Key studies

  1. Systematic review20043,329 menopausal women in 24 randomized trials

    Oral oestrogen and combined oestrogen/progestogen therapy versus placebo for hot flushes

    Hormone therapy cut weekly hot flushes by about 75% versus placebo and made them less severe. 5

  2. Meta-analysis2019108,647 postmenopausal women who developed breast cancer

    Type and timing of menopausal hormone therapy and breast cancer risk: individual participant meta-analysis of the worldwide epidemiological evidence

    All types of hormone therapy except vaginal estrogen were linked to more breast cancer, more so for estrogen plus progestogen and for longer use; some excess risk lasted over 10 years after stopping. 9

  3. RCT201327,347 postmenopausal women aged 50 to 79

    Menopausal hormone therapy and health outcomes during the intervention and extended poststopping phases of the Women's Health Initiative randomized trials

    Conjugated estrogens with or without a progestin relieved hot flashes and cut hip fractures but raised stroke and clot risk, and the combined pill raised breast cancer; the authors did not support hormone therapy for preventing chronic disease. 6

  4. RCT2016643 healthy postmenopausal women

    Vascular effects of early versus late postmenopausal treatment with estradiol

    Oral estradiol slowed artery-wall thickening when started within 6 years of menopause but not 10 or more years after; heart CT measures did not differ. 7

Questions for your clinician

  • Given my age and years since menopause, do the benefits outweigh the risks for me?
  • Would a patch or gel be safer for me than a tablet, given my clot and stroke risk?
  • Do I need a progestogen to protect my uterus, and what bleeding should I report?

Status in the US

FDA-approved FDA-approved prescription hormone. Approved for menopausal hot flashes, vaginal atrophy, low estrogen from hypogonadism or ovarian failure, osteoporosis prevention and some cancer palliation. In 2025-2026 FDA had makers remove the heart, breast-cancer and dementia statements from the boxed warning (estrogen-alone products keep an endometrial-cancer warning), and labels are being updated product by product. 1234
