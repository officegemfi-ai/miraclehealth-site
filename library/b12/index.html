Vitamin B12

Also called Methylcobalamin, Cyanocobalamin, Hydroxocobalamin, B12 shots

What it is

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is needed to make healthy red blood cells and DNA. The central nervous system needs it to develop, to form myelin and to work. Stomach acid and enzymes release B12 from food. It then joins with intrinsic factor and is absorbed in the last part of the small intestine (the ileum). Cyanocobalamin is the most common supplement form. No evidence shows that other forms, such as methylcobalamin, are absorbed differently.

Strong evidence

Treating a deficiency is standard care. Small trials show that B12 by mouth and by injection correct low levels about equally well. Trials in older adults show no improvement in thinking skills. NIH finds no performance benefit without a deficiency.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Corrects vitamin B12 deficiency, which can cause fatigue, megaloblastic anemia and nerve damage that may appear even without anemia 1

  • Moderate evidence

    Oral B12 normalized blood levels about as well as injections in small trials, at lower cost 23

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    No upper limit has been set because B12 is considered safe even at high doses; studies linking high intake or blood levels to cancer are mixed and unsettled 1

  • Not known

    Side effects are poorly reported in trials (very low certainty evidence); in one trial 2 of 30 people stopped oral B12 early because of adverse events 2

Who should skip it, or ask a doctor first

  • Numbness, tingling, balance problems or memory changes: get tested before self-treating, since nerve damage can occur without anemia
  • Pernicious anemia, or past stomach or bowel surgery such as weight-loss surgery: B12 is usually needed, under a doctor's care
  • Regular use of metformin, proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers: ask a doctor to check your B12 level

What it can clash with

  • Metformin: may reduce B12 absorption and significantly lower blood B12 levels
  • Proton pump inhibitors and H2 blockers: can reduce absorption of B12 from food by slowing stomach acid release

Blood tests to watch

Before starting

  • Serum or plasma vitamin B12
  • Methylmalonic acid, which NIH describes as among the most sensitive markers of B12 status
  • Homocysteine, which rises with low B12 but is less specific

While taking it

  • Serum B12 to confirm that levels have normalized

Serum B12 is the usual test and methylmalonic acid the most sensitive marker; older adults, vegans and people on metformin or long-term acid reducers are more likely to run low.

The main studies

  1. Systematic review2018153 people with B12 deficiency in 3 randomized trials

    Oral vitamin B12 versus intramuscular vitamin B12 for vitamin B12 deficiency

    Oral and intramuscular B12 normalized blood levels similarly over 3 to 4 months, and oral treatment cost less; the evidence was low certainty and no trial measured symptoms. 2

  2. Review2022Trials in adults, including a noninferiority trial in adults over 65

    Oral vs. Intramuscular Vitamin B12 for Treating Vitamin B12 Deficiency

    Concluded that oral and intramuscular B12 are equivalent for normalizing blood levels after 1 to 4 months. 3

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Should my B12 be checked, given my diet, age or medicines like metformin or acid reducers?
  • Would pills work for me, or do I need injections?
  • If my B12 result is borderline, should we check methylmalonic acid?

Where it stands in the US

Dietary supplement Supplement; injections and nasal gel by prescription. Sold as a dietary supplement. Vitamin B12 is also a prescription medicine, usually given by intramuscular injection, and is available as a prescription nasal gel spray. 1
