What it is

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin found naturally in few foods, added to others such as milk and breakfast cereals, and made in the skin when it is exposed to UVB sunlight. Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is the form made in skin and found in animal foods, while D2 comes from mushrooms and yeast; D3 raises blood levels more, and keeps them up longer, than D2. The liver converts vitamin D to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the form measured in blood tests, and the kidneys turn that into the active hormone, which helps the gut absorb calcium and supports bone growth, muscle and immune function.

Strong evidence

Strong for preventing and treating vitamin D deficiency. For other uses, large randomized trials, including VITAL with 25,871 adults, found that supplements given to people not selected for deficiency did not prevent fractures, falls, cancer, heart disease or depression, though pooled trials show fewer cancer deaths.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Prevents rickets in children and the bone-softening disease osteomalacia in adults 12

  • Moderate evidence

    Possibly lower risk of death in adults 75 and older, the reason the Endocrine Society suggests supplements for this age group 2

  • Moderate evidence

    Lower risk of progressing from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes in pooled trials of moderate to higher doses, although a trial of 2,423 adults with prediabetes did not show a significant effect 312

  • Moderate evidence

    About 12% to 13% fewer cancer deaths in meta-analyses of trials, with no change in how many people develop cancer 1

  • Limited evidence

    May reduce respiratory infections in children and teens, and possibly preeclampsia, preterm birth and newborn deaths when taken in pregnancy 2

Risks and side effects

  • Rare

    Too much vitamin D, almost always from supplements rather than sun, raises blood calcium, which can cause nausea, vomiting, muscle weakness and kidney stones and, in extreme cases, kidney failure and irregular heartbeat 13

  • Not known

    Kidney stones were 17% more common over 7 years when vitamin D was combined with calcium in a trial of 36,282 postmenopausal women 1

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • History of kidney stones, especially if also taking calcium (ask a clinician first)
  • Taking orlistat, statins, steroids or thiazide diuretics (check with a clinician first)

Interactions

  • Orlistat (weight-loss drug): can reduce absorption of vitamin D from food and supplements
  • Statins: large vitamin D intakes may weaken atorvastatin, lovastatin and simvastatin, and statins may lower the body's own vitamin D production; evidence is limited, and statin doses are for the prescriber to change
  • Steroids such as prednisone, and thiazide diuretics, are also listed by NIH as medicines that interact with vitamin D

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

None routinely needed.

While taking it

None routinely needed.

The marker that matters is blood 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], the main measure of vitamin D status, but the Endocrine Society advises against routine testing in healthy people, including those with darker skin or obesity, because levels that improve outcomes have not been established in trials.

Key studies

  1. Guideline2024General population groups by age, pregnancy and prediabetes

    Vitamin D for the Prevention of Disease: An Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline

    Suggests supplements for ages 1 to 18, adults 75 and older, pregnancy and high-risk prediabetes; suggests against supplementing beyond usual intake in healthy adults under 75 and against routine 25(OH)D testing; sets no target blood level. 2

Questions for your clinician

  • Am I in a group where vitamin D is suggested, such as being 75 or older, pregnant or having prediabetes?
  • Is a vitamin D blood test useful for me, or not needed?
  • Do my medicines or calcium supplements change whether vitamin D is right for me?

Status in the US

Dietary supplement Dietary supplement. Sold as a dietary supplement and added to foods. The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline suggests supplements for children and teens, adults 75 and older, pregnancy and high-risk prediabetes, and advises against routine blood testing in healthy people. 12
