What it is

GHRP-2 (pralmorelin) is a synthetic peptide of six amino acids, including one not found in nature, from a family of growth hormone-releasing peptides originally derived from enkephalin, a natural opioid-like peptide. It acts on the ghrelin receptor to prompt the pituitary gland to release growth hormone, and it also raises appetite and cortisol. In Japan it is used clinically as a diagnostic agent for growth hormone deficiency; it has no approved use in the US.

Limited evidence

Small, short human studies show growth hormone release, more appetite and normal growth rates in children with growth hormone deficiency; a 5-day course in nine healthy men did not raise IGF-1, and there are no long-term trials.

What it may do

  • Limited evidence

    Normal growth rates, though no catch-up growth, in short children with growth hormone deficiency given a nasal form 67

  • Limited evidence

    More appetite and food intake in lean and obese adults 6

  • Limited evidence

    Normalized IGF-1 in people with prolonged critical illness when infused together with GHRH 67

  • Limited evidence

    Higher IGF-1 in 14 men on testosterone therapy who took it with sermorelin and GHRP-6 7

  • Limited evidence

    Weight gain and more hunger in one woman with severe, long-standing anorexia nervosa 8

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    FDA cites reports of serious adverse events, including higher insulin needs, deaths of critically ill study subjects, infection and pancreatitis; causality not established 1

  • Not known

    Increased appetite with weight gain, and higher cortisol, which a review calls built into how ghrelin-type drugs work 9

  • Not known

    Possible immune reactions from aggregation and impurities in injectable or nasal compounded products, per FDA 1

  • Not known

    Long-term safety data are lacking for growth hormone secretagogues, with some concern about raised blood sugar 6

  • Not known

    Black-market supply: a review reports ghrelin mimetics are sold on the black market, so identity and purity are uncertain 9

Talk to a clinician first if you are

  • Critical illness (deaths were reported in critically ill study subjects)
  • Diabetes or insulin use (clinician first: higher insulin needs reported)
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (no safety data)
  • Competitive athletes (prohibited by WADA at all times)

Interactions

  • Insulin and other diabetes medicines: FDA has reports of increased insulin requirements in people given GHRP-2

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

  • General health check with a clinician
  • Fasting glucose or HbA1c
  • IGF-1

While taking it

  • Fasting glucose or HbA1c
  • IGF-1

No evidence-based monitoring exists for GHRP-2; a review of growth hormone secretagogues advises tracking IGF-1, blood sugar and HbA1c, and FDA has reports of rising insulin needs.

Key studies

  1. Review2018Review of human studies of growth hormone secretagogues

    The Safety and Efficacy of Growth Hormone Secretagogues

    GHRP-2 increased growth rates in short children, increased appetite in lean and obese adults and normalized IGF-1 in prolonged critical illness. The authors flag rising blood sugar and missing long-term safety data for the class. 6

  2. Case series201714 men on testosterone therapy (retrospective chart review)

    Growth Hormone Secretagogue Treatment in Hypogonadal Men Raises Serum Insulin-Like Growth Factor-1 Levels

    A combination of GHRP-2, GHRP-6 and sermorelin raised average IGF-1 from about 160 to 239 ng/mL; the authors note an earlier 5-day study in nine healthy young men showed no IGF-1 rise with GHRP-2 alone. 7

  3. Case report2015One 38-year-old woman with severe anorexia nervosa

    One-year intranasal application of growth hormone releasing peptide-2 improves body weight and hypoglycemia in a severely emaciated anorexia nervosa patient

    Over 14 months, body weight rose from 21.1 to 27.8 kg with more hunger, less fatigue and fewer low-blood-sugar episodes; no obvious side effects were reported. 8

  4. Review2012Review of published research

    Growth Hormone Doping in Sports: A Critical Review of Use and Detection Strategies

    Nasal or oral GHRP-2 roughly doubled growth hormone secretion in children without changing IGF-1; higher cortisol and appetite are intrinsic side effects of the drug class. 9

Questions for your clinician

  • Would the appetite and cortisol effects work against my goals?
  • How would we watch my blood sugar and IGF-1, especially if I have diabetes risk?
  • Is there an approved treatment for my actual goal or diagnosis?

Status in the US

FDA safety-risk list Not approved; on FDA's safety-risk list (503B). FDA lists injectable and nasal GHRP-2 in Category 2 (significant safety risks) for outsourcing facilities and places GHRP-2 in Category 3 (nominated without adequate support) for ordinary compounding pharmacies, which leaves compounding open to FDA enforcement. WADA bans it in sport. 12345
