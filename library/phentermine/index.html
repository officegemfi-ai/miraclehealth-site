What it is

Phentermine, sold under brand names including Adipex-P, is a prescription appetite suppressant. Its label describes it as a sympathomimetic amine, chemically and pharmacologically related to amphetamine. It is approved only for short-term use (a few weeks) as an add-on to a weight-reduction program in people with a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or more, or 27 or more with other risk factors.

Strong evidence

It is FDA-approved for short-term weight loss; evidence on longer use comes mainly from observational health-record studies, not long-term randomized trials.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Short-term weight loss as an add-on to a weight-reduction program in people with a BMI of 30 or more, or 27 or more with other risk factors (approved use) 1

  • Limited evidence

    Off-label longer use (the label approves only short-term use, a few weeks): among 13,972 adults in US health systems, those who took it continuously for more than a year lost 7.4% more of their body weight at 2 years than short-term users; this kind of study cannot prove cause, and weight came back after stopping 2

  • Limited evidence

    Slightly lower systolic blood pressure at 2 years in longer-term (off-label) users in that study, which the authors linked to greater weight loss 2

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    Heart and blood pressure: raised blood pressure, palpitations, a fast heart rate and ischemic events such as heart attacks are listed side effects; the label rules it out in people with a history of heart disease and urges caution even with mild high blood pressure; one health-record study of longer use found no significant rise in heart attacks, strokes or deaths, but only 41 occurred, too few to rule out harm 12

  • Rare

    Primary pulmonary hypertension, a rare and frequently fatal lung-artery disease, and heart valve disease; valve disease has been reported rarely with phentermine alone, and new, unexplained shortness of breath needs prompt medical review 1

  • Not known

    Overstimulation, restlessness, insomnia, dizziness and tremor; psychosis has also been reported 1

  • Not known

    Misuse and dependence: it is related to amphetamine and is a Schedule IV controlled substance; stopping after long, high-dose use can cause extreme tiredness and depression 1

  • Not known

    The appetite effect can wear off as tolerance develops 1

  • Not known

    It can impair driving and other hazardous tasks, and alcohol may cause adverse reactions 1

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • History of heart disease, including coronary artery disease, stroke, irregular heart rhythm, heart failure or uncontrolled high blood pressure (contraindicated)
  • Taking an MAO inhibitor now or within the past 14 days (contraindicated)
  • Overactive thyroid, glaucoma, agitated states or a history of drug misuse (contraindicated)
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (contraindicated)
  • Athletes in tested sports (WADA bans phentermine in competition)

Interactions

  • MAO inhibitors: risk of a dangerous rise in blood pressure (hypertensive crisis); not to be used together or within 14 days
  • Other weight-loss drugs, and serotonergic drugs such as SSRI antidepressants: the label says combining them is not recommended
  • Alcohol: may cause adverse reactions
  • Insulin and other diabetes medicines: requirements may change, which the prescriber manages
  • Adrenergic neuron-blocking blood pressure drugs: their effect may be reduced

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

None routinely needed.

While taking it

  • Blood glucose in people with diabetes, whose medicine needs may change

The label focuses on heart and blood-pressure history rather than blood tests; people with diabetes may need closer glucose checks.

Key studies

  1. Cohort201913,972 adults aged 18 to 64 in US integrated health systems

    Safety and Effectiveness of Longer-Term Phentermine Use: Clinical Outcomes from an Electronic Health Record Cohort

    Compared with short-term use, more than a year of continuous use was linked with 7.4% more weight loss at 2 years and no significant difference in heart events or death (41 events in total). The design was observational, and the authors called for randomized trials. 2

  2. Label2024People with a BMI of 30 or more, or 27 or more with other risk factors

    ADIPEX-P (phentermine hydrochloride): prescribing information

    Approved for short-term use only; ruled out with heart disease, MAO inhibitors, overactive thyroid, glaucoma, agitated states, a history of drug misuse, pregnancy and nursing. 1

Questions for your clinician

  • Are my heart and blood pressure healthy enough for phentermine?
  • How long would I take it, and what is the plan for keeping weight off afterward?
  • Would a medicine approved for long-term weight management suit me better?

Status in the US

FDA-approved FDA-approved for short-term weight loss; Schedule IV. Adipex-P is approved only as a short-term (a few weeks) add-on in a weight-reduction program for people with a BMI of 30 or more, or 27 or more with other risk factors. It is a Schedule IV controlled substance, and longer-term use is off-label. 1
