What it is

BPC-157 is a 15-amino-acid peptide, a fragment of a protein called body protection compound that was first isolated from human gastric juice. In rodents it has helped injured tendons, gut lining and other tissues heal; researchers suspect it works partly by encouraging new blood vessels and nitric oxide signaling, but FDA says its mechanisms remain poorly understood. No country has approved a medicine containing it.

Limited evidence

Most data come from rodent studies; the handful of human studies are tiny, uncontrolled or published only as meeting abstracts, and FDA judged them insufficient to establish safety or effectiveness. The tendon, muscle and other tissue benefits come only from animal studies.

What it may do

  • Animal and lab studies

    Faster healing of cut tendons in rodents 1

  • Animal and lab studies

    Healing of surgically created colon fistulas and protection against stomach and intestinal damage from NSAID painkillers in rats 1

  • Limited evidence

    In one placebo-controlled trial in 53 people with mild to moderate ulcerative colitis, BPC-157 enemas lowered a disease activity score more than placebo; it was reported only as a meeting abstract, and FDA judged the data inadequate 1

  • Animal and lab studies

    Protection in rodent models of stroke, encephalopathy (brain dysfunction) and spinal cord injury 1

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    Too little human safety information to describe its side effects: the human studies were too small to show them 1

  • In theory

    Immune reactions, which FDA called a possibly significant risk that peptide clumping could make worse 12

  • In theory

    Unknown cancer risk: no cancer studies exist, and it may promote new blood vessel growth 1

  • Not known

    Changed blood-clotting tests in rats and dogs, plus higher liver enzyme (ALT), glucose and triglyceride levels in animal toxicity studies 1

  • Not known

    Reports to FDA after injections, including injection-site swelling, shortness of breath leading to an ER visit, and skin and gum darkening; cause not established 1

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (not studied in people)
  • Current or past cancer, unless a clinician advises otherwise (possible blood vessel growth effects; no cancer studies)
  • Bleeding or clotting disorders, or taking blood thinners (clotting tests changed in animal studies)
  • Athletes subject to anti-doping rules (BPC-157 is banned by WADA)

Interactions

  • None well documented

Blood markers to watch

Not an approved medicine. These are checks a clinician or a clinical trial would use, not a plan for taking it on your own.

Before starting

  • Complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel (includes liver enzymes and glucose) as a general health check

While taking it

None routinely needed.

No evidence-based monitoring exists for BPC-157; a general check is a reasonable baseline, and animal studies flagged liver enzyme, glucose and clotting changes.

Key studies

  1. Review2026All published human and animal studies, including 5 small human studies

    FDA found too little clinical safety information to characterize BPC-157's safety and no adequate evidence that it treats ulcerative colitis. It found no human data on how the body absorbs it by mouth, injection under the skin, nose or skin. 1

Status in the US

Under FDA review Not approved; compounding not yet allowed. FDA took BPC-157 off its Category 2 safety-risk list in April 2026, and in July 2026 its compounding advisory committee voted 8-6 (1 abstention) to recommend allowing pharmacy compounding, against FDA staff advice. As of mid-October 2026 FDA had not added it to the compounding list or issued an interim policy, so compounding was still not permitted. 1234
