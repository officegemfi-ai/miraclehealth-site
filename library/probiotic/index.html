What it is

Probiotics are live microorganisms that provide a health benefit when given in adequate amounts. Common ones include Bifidobacterium, members of the Lactobacillaceae family such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, and the yeast Saccharomyces boulardii. Effects are specific to the strain. Products are measured in colony-forming units (CFUs), though a higher count does not mean a product works better.

Moderate evidence

Cochrane reviews of dozens of randomized trials give moderate-certainty evidence that probiotics prevent antibiotic-associated and C. difficile diarrhea. The evidence for IBS and other uses is weaker and depends on the strain.

What it may do

  • Moderate evidence

    Fewer cases of C. difficile diarrhea in people taking antibiotics (1.5% vs 4.0%), mainly where baseline risk is above 5% 2

  • Moderate evidence

    Fewer cases of antibiotic-associated diarrhea in children (8% vs 19% across 33 trials); L. rhamnosus and S. boulardii looked most promising 31

  • Moderate evidence

    Saccharomyces boulardii reduced antibiotic-associated diarrhea in adults and children across 21 trials (4,780 people) 1

  • Limited evidence

    Might reduce some IBS symptoms, with modest benefits in pooled trials 1

  • Limited evidence

    Might modestly reduce disease activity in mild to moderate ulcerative colitis 1

Risks and side effects

  • Uncommon

    Minor effects such as gas, bloating, nausea, constipation or rash; in children's trials, side effects were no more common than in control groups 31

  • Rare

    Serious bloodstream infections (bacteremia or fungemia), mostly in people who are severely ill or immunocompromised or who have a central venous catheter 13

  • Rare

    Preterm infants: FDA reported one death and more than two dozen other adverse events and warns of invasive, potentially fatal disease 1

  • Not known

    Products may not contain the strains listed, and labels are only required to give the total weight of microorganisms, which says nothing about how many are alive 1

Who should skip it, or ask a doctor first

  • Weakened immune system or serious illness (doctor first)
  • Central venous catheter
  • Critically ill patients in intensive care
  • Preterm infants

What it can clash with

  • None well documented

The main studies

  1. Systematic review2025Adults and children taking antibiotics

    Probiotics for the prevention of Clostridioides difficile-associated diarrhea in adults and children

    Probiotics cut C. difficile diarrhea from 4.0% to 1.5% (moderate certainty), with the benefit concentrated in trials where baseline risk exceeded 5%; they were likely not harmful in people who were not immunocompromised. 2

  2. Systematic review20196,352 children taking antibiotics in 33 trials

    Probiotics for the prevention of antibiotic-associated diarrhea in children

    Antibiotic-associated diarrhea fell from 19% to 8%, and no serious adverse events were attributed to probiotics. 3

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Would a probiotic help prevent diarrhea with the antibiotic I'm starting, and which strain has evidence?
  • Does my health or immune status make probiotics unsafe for me?
  • For my IBS symptoms, is a probiotic worth trying, and how will we judge whether it works?

Where it stands in the US

Dietary supplement Dietary supplement. Sold as dietary supplements in capsules, powders, liquids and other forms. FDA says these products have not gone through its premarket review of safety and effectiveness for medical uses, and in 2023 it warned that giving probiotics to preterm infants may cause serious, potentially fatal infections. 1
