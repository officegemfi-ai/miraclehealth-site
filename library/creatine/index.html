What it is

Creatine is made in the body by the liver and kidneys from the amino acids glycine, arginine and methionine, and small amounts also come from animal foods. Most of it is stored in skeletal and heart muscle, partly as phosphocreatine, which muscles use to regenerate ATP, the cell's energy molecule, at the start of intense effort. Supplements raise the amount of creatine stored in muscle.

Strong evidence

Meta-analyses of dozens of randomized trials show greater strength and lean-mass gains when creatine is added to resistance training, in both younger and older adults.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Larger gains in upper- and lower-body strength than resistance training alone in adults under 50, based on 23 randomized trials (mostly in men) 2

  • Strong evidence

    Better performance in repeated short bursts of high-intensity activity such as sprinting and weightlifting; little value for endurance sports 13

  • Moderate evidence

    More lean mass (about 1.3 kg on average) and small extra strength gains when added to resistance training in adults over 50 4

Risks and side effects

  • Common

    Weight gain, mainly from water retention 1

  • Not known

    Raises blood creatinine, so standard kidney tests (creatinine and eGFR) can look worse than they are even when the kidneys are healthy 5

  • Not known

    Very little safety evidence in people who already have kidney disease; reviewers advise people with very low kidney function to avoid it 5

  • Uncommon

    Nausea, diarrhea, muscle cramps, stiffness and heat intolerance have been reported anecdotally; in trials in older adults, stomach upset and cramps were about as common as with placebo 14

  • Not known

    Contaminated products may pose risks, so product purity matters 5

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Kidney disease or reduced kidney function
  • Teenagers: pediatric and high-school professional associations advise against performance supplements
  • Older adults or people with chronic medical conditions planning long-term use (have kidney function checked with a clinician first)

Interactions

  • None well documented

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

  • Kidney function (creatinine with eGFR, or cystatin C) for older adults or people with medical conditions planning long-term use

While taking it

  • Periodic kidney function checks in those higher-risk groups during long-term use; cystatin C or a measured GFR avoids the creatinine effect

Creatine raises blood creatinine without harming healthy kidneys, so creatinine-based kidney estimates can look worse than they are; tell your clinician and the lab that you take it.

Key studies

  1. Meta-analysis2024Adults under 50 in 23 randomized trials (20 enrolled only men)

    Effects of Creatine Supplementation and Resistance Training on Muscle Strength Gains in Adults <50 Years of Age: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

    Creatine plus resistance training added about 4.4 kg to upper-body and 11.4 kg to lower-body strength gains versus placebo; gains in women were not statistically significant, but only three trials included women. 2

  2. Meta-analysis2021509 adults with an average age over 50 in 16 randomized trials (lean mass analysis)

    Meta-Analysis Examining the Importance of Creatine Ingestion Strategies on Lean Tissue Mass and Strength in Older Adults

    Creatine with resistance training added about 1.3 kg of lean mass, small gains in chest-press strength and borderline gains in leg-press strength versus placebo; several authors disclosed ties to creatine companies. 4

  3. Review2017Review of studies in athletes, healthy adults and patient groups

    International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine

    Concluded that creatine improves high-intensity exercise performance and training adaptations, and that short- and long-term use is safe and well tolerated in healthy people and in a number of patient groups. 3

  4. Review2023Review of controlled trials, a small meta-analysis and case reports

    Is It Time for a Requiem for Creatine Supplementation-Induced Kidney Failure? A Narrative Review

    Controlled trials do not show kidney harm in healthy people; creatine raises creatinine and can mislead kidney tests, and safety evidence in people with kidney disease is very limited. 5

Questions for your clinician

  • Should my kidney function be checked before I start, and should future tests use cystatin C because creatine raises creatinine?
  • Is creatine reasonable for me given my age, kidney health and training goals?
  • Could water weight from creatine affect anything you track, such as my weight or lab results?

Status in the US

Dietary supplement Dietary supplement. Sold as a dietary supplement. NIH's Office of Dietary Supplements says it is considered safe for short-term use by healthy adults and that evidence shows use for several years is safe. 1
