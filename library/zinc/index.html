What it is

Zinc is an essential mineral required for the activity of hundreds of enzymes and involved in immune function, protein and DNA synthesis, wound healing and cell signaling. Deficiency can delay growth, impair taste and smell, slow wound healing, and cause hair loss and frequent infections. Besides supplements, zinc is found in cold lozenges, some nasal sprays and some denture adhesive creams.

Limited evidence

Many trials suggest zinc may shorten colds by about 2 days, but a 2024 Cochrane review rated the evidence low to very low certainty; a large trial supports a zinc-containing formula for macular degeneration.

What it may do

  • Limited evidence

    May shorten colds by about 2 days in people who already have one, with little to no effect on preventing colds (34 trials, about 8,500 people) 31

  • Moderate evidence

    Slows progression to advanced age-related macular degeneration in people at high risk when taken in a formula with copper and antioxidants (25% lower risk in the AREDS trial of 4,757 people) 1

  • Moderate evidence

    Shortens acute diarrhea in children in low-income countries; the effect may be marginal in well-nourished children 1

  • Limited evidence

    Some trials show better blood sugar, cholesterol or insulin resistance, but results conflict and evidence is insufficient for diabetes prevention 1

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    Altered taste and stomach upset with zinc lozenges, more often than with placebo 3

  • Not known

    Nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, dizziness, headaches and stomach upset from excess zinc 1

  • Rare

    Copper deficiency with long-term high intake, which can cause anemia and nerve damage; overuse of zinc-containing denture creams has caused this 1

  • Not known

    Long-term high intake can also weaken immune function and lower HDL (good) cholesterol 1

  • Rare

    Loss of sense of smell, possibly permanent, reported with nasal zinc gels and swabs 2

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Anyone considering zinc nasal gels, swabs or sprays
  • Taking penicillamine, whose absorption and action zinc can reduce (clinician first)
  • Planning long-term use above NIH's upper limit, which applies to people not under medical treatment with zinc (clinician first)

Interactions

  • Quinolone antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin (Cipro) and tetracycline antibiotics: zinc can reduce absorption of both
  • Penicillamine: zinc can reduce its absorption and action
  • Thiazide diuretics: increase zinc loss in urine
  • Iron supplements taken at the same time can reduce zinc absorption

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

None routinely needed.

While taking it

None routinely needed.

No routine tests are needed; blood zinc is the usual test, but it does not always match intake and shifts with infection, age, sex and time of day.

Key studies

  1. Systematic review2024About 8,500 adults and children in 34 placebo-controlled studies, mostly in the US

    Zinc for prevention and treatment of the common cold

    Zinc might shorten colds by about 2 days but had little to no effect on preventing them; altered taste and stomach upset were more common with zinc, and many studies had unclear or high risk of bias. 3

Questions for your clinician

  • Am I already getting enough zinc from food and other supplements, so I don't take too much?
  • Does zinc interact with my antibiotics or other medicines?
  • If I take zinc long term, should my copper level be checked?

Status in the US

Dietary supplement Dietary supplement. Sold as a dietary supplement and in cold lozenges. In 2009 FDA told consumers to stop using three Zicam nasal zinc products after more than 130 reports of lost sense of smell; the warning did not cover zinc tablets or lozenges taken by mouth. 12
