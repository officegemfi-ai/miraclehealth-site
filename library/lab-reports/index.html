Guide

How to read a lab report (certificate of analysis)

For adults who already have a peptide, hormone or supplement product and want to understand its lab report. This guide doesn’t recommend buying these products, and no report can make one safe.

Checked Oct 29, 2026 against 24 sources · clinician review pending

What a certificate of analysis is

A certificate of analysis (COA) is a lab’s report on tests of a sample from one production batch. The World Health Organization’s model COA includes the lab’s name and address, a unique report number, the batch number, the date the sample arrived, each test’s method, limit and result, a pass-or-fail conclusion, and the dated signature of the person who approved it 1. ISO/IEC 17025, the international standard for testing labs, adds that a report should state that its results apply only to the items tested 2.

An in-house report comes from the maker’s or seller’s own lab; an independent (third-party) one comes from a lab with no stake in the result. Independent is better, but either describes only the sample sent in, which the seller may pick. Even FDA-regulated drug makers can’t rely on a supplier’s report alone: federal rules require their own identity test on every ingredient 3, and FDA has cited companies for reports with copied results or no records behind them 4.

What a credible peptide report shows

  • Identity, by mass spectrometry. The measured mass should match the expected mass of the exact molecule named. Names aren’t standardized: a 2012 analysis found that one “TB-500” product held a 7-amino-acid fragment of thymosin beta-4, under a fifth of the full protein’s mass 5, and FDA has encountered multiple salts and derivatives of BPC-157 6.
  • Purity, by HPLC. The percentage of detected material that is the target peptide 7, ideally with the chromatogram (the trace of peaks) attached. For generic peptide drugs, FDA expects any peptide-related impurity at 0.10% or more to be identified, since impurities may affect immune responses, and encourages more than one test method 8.
  • Quantity per vial, ideally as net peptide content. The powder isn’t all peptide. It often includes counter-ions left from manufacturing, such as trifluoroacetate (TFA) or acetate, plus water, and both should be measured 9. In one 2025 study, TFA made up as much as 35% of a peptide salt’s weight 10.
  • Sterility and bacterial endotoxin, for anything injected. Endotoxins are fever-causing pieces of bacterial cell walls, measured with the LAL (Limulus amebocyte lysate) test or a newer recombinant version 711. FDA calls endotoxin testing “a critical quality attribute” for BPC-157 6. Look for numbers with units and limits, not just “pass.”
  • Heavy metals and residual solvents, where relevant. These need their own tests; a purity result doesn’t cover them 67.
  • The details that tie it together. Batch or lot number, sample and test dates, the lab’s name, address and accreditation (for example, ISO/IEC 17025), method references, and a named, signed reviewer 12.

How to check that a report is genuine

  1. Match the lot. The batch or lot number on the report should match your vial or bottle 1.
  2. Find the lab yourself, not through the seller’s link. If it claims accreditation, the accreditation body can confirm its status and scope 12, which should cover the specific test reported 1.
  3. Ask the lab whether it issued that report number. Industry experts likewise advise drug buyers to confirm a COA copy is genuine 4.

Red flags

  • No batch or lot number, or one that doesn’t match your vial
  • One report reused for every batch, or impossible dates (tested before the batch was made)
  • Results that look too tidy: round numbers everywhere, or identical figures across batches
  • A purity figure with no chromatogram or mass spectrum behind it
  • A lab you can’t find or reach, or accreditation you can’t confirm
  • No named, signed reviewer
  • Mismatched fonts, blurred patches or other signs of editing
  • A “COA” that’s just a badge or table on the seller’s own page. Advertised numbers can be far off: in one study, vials advertised as 99% pure measured 7% to 14% 13.

What even a good report can’t tell you

  • It describes the sample tested, not your vial 2. As the European Pharmacopoeia notes, a passing sterility test “only indicates that no contaminating micro-organism has been found in the sample examined” 14.
  • High purity doesn’t mean clean. In a large 2026 dataset, purity didn’t predict endotoxin contamination 7.
  • If sterility and endotoxin aren’t on the report, nothing shows an injectable is free of microbes or endotoxin. And sterile isn’t endotoxin-free: sterilizing filters don’t remove endotoxins 11, and in one study a vial with no living microbes still had elevated endotoxin 13. In FDA’s 2026 review of BPC-157, most COAs it saw reported purity only, and those submitted to it had no endotoxin or microbial-load results 6. Also in 2026, at least 30 people had reactions such as fever, chills and signs of shock after compounded IV glutathione made with a supplement-grade ingredient; FDA said the symptoms were consistent with excess endotoxin and was investigating 15.
  • Storage and shipping matter. Peptides can break down with heat, light, pH changes and agitation, and most peptide drugs need cold storage and transport 16. A report can’t show what happened after testing. FDA recommends not using any injectable GLP-1 drug that arrives warm or poorly refrigerated 17.
  • “Research use only” means not made for people. FDA has warned companies over unapproved drugs, including semaglutide, tirzepatide and retatrutide, falsely labeled “for research purposes” or “not for human consumption,” and urges consumers not to buy these products of unknown quality 17.
  • A report isn’t approval, legal permission or proof of safety. BPC-157, for example, isn’t part of any FDA-approved drug, no country has approved a product containing it, and FDA found too little clinical safety information to characterize its safety 6. Each compound’s Library page shows its current US status.

What testing has found

  • Peptides submitted by consumers and vendors (2026 preprint). Across more than 6,000 results for 14 peptides, 42% to 71% failed basic quality benchmarks (depending on how strict), most often because the amount differed from the label. About 1 in 10 TB-500 samples lacked the stated compound, and 15% of the 243 samples tested for endotoxin had measurable levels. The study isn’t yet peer reviewed, and its samples were volunteered, not randomly bought 7.
  • Semaglutide bought online without a prescription (2024). Of six orders, three never arrived. The three vials that did held 29% to 39% more semaglutide than labeled, and one had elevated endotoxin 13.
  • Anabolic steroids (2022). A review of 19 studies covering 5,413 black-market samples, mostly seized by authorities, estimated that 36% were counterfeit and another 37% substandard 18.
  • Supplements (2018). FDA warnings from 2007 to 2016 identified unapproved drug ingredients in 776 supplements, mostly sold for sexual enhancement, weight loss or muscle building 19.

Supplements: what certification marks mean

Dietary supplements don’t need FDA approval before they’re sold. Independent seals indicate that a product was properly made and doesn’t contain harmful levels of contaminants, but they “do not guarantee that a product is safe or effective” 20.

  • USP Verified: listed ingredients in the declared amounts, no harmful levels of specified contaminants, proper breakdown in the body, and good manufacturing practices 21, checked with facility audits and testing of products bought off the shelf 22.
  • NSF Certified for Sport: label review, lot-by-lot testing for banned substances and unannounced facility inspections. NSF says it “helps minimize the risk” of banned substances 23.
  • Informed Sport: every batch is tested for prohibited substances before release, though the program notes that no testing covers every one 24.

Check the program’s website, not just the logo; Informed Sport, for example, recommends searching your batch number 24. These marks cover supplements, not peptide vials.

What to bring to a clinician

  • The vial or bottle, with its label and lot number
  • The report, and what the lab said when you checked it
  • The original packaging and any paperwork that came with it
  • A list of everything else you take, including medicines and supplements 20

If you feel unwell after using an injectable product, with fever, chills, dizziness or faintness, stop using it and get medical care promptly. Keep the vial, and report the problem to FDA’s MedWatch program 17. Lot numbers matter: FDA’s 2026 glutathione alert named the ingredient lot involved 15.
