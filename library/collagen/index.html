What it is

Collagen is a structural protein of skin, cartilage and other connective tissue. Collagen peptide supplements (hydrolyzed collagen) contain collagen broken down into small fragments, often made from fish skin or scales or other animal sources, and are taken by mouth. They have been tested mainly for skin aging and for osteoarthritis symptoms.

Limited evidence

Many randomized trials report better skin hydration and elasticity, but a 2025 meta-analysis found the benefit only in industry-funded or lower-quality trials, not in independent or high-quality ones; joint evidence is a meta-analysis with mixed results.

What it may do

  • Limited evidence

    Better skin hydration, elasticity and wrinkles when 23 randomized trials (1,474 people) were pooled, but no effect in trials without industry funding or in high-quality trials 2

  • Limited evidence

    Better skin hydration and brightness and a modest effect on wrinkles in a 2026 pooling of 19 trials (1,341 people), mostly of oral collagen peptides, with very large differences between trials 3

  • Limited evidence

    Lower overall osteoarthritis symptom scores and less stiffness than placebo in people with osteoarthritis, though pain and function subscores did not improve 4

Risks and side effects

  • Rare

    Few side effects reported in trials and no severe ones; mild stomach discomfort occurred in isolated cases 3

  • Not known

    Not reviewed by FDA for safety or effectiveness before it is sold 1

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Allergy to fish or another animal source the product is made from (check the label)

Interactions

  • None well documented

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

None routinely needed.

While taking it

None routinely needed.

No blood tests are needed for collagen supplements; trials judged results by skin measurements and joint symptom scores, not lab markers.

Key studies

  1. Meta-analysis20251,474 participants in 23 randomized trials

    Effects of Collagen Supplements on Skin Aging: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials

    Pooled results showed better hydration, elasticity and wrinkles, but trials without industry funding and high-quality trials showed no effect; the authors concluded there is no clinical evidence to support collagen for preventing or treating skin aging. 2

  2. Meta-analysis20261,341 participants in 19 randomized trials (17 oral, 2 topical)

    Oral and topical peptides for skin aging: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials

    Peptides, mostly oral collagen, were linked to better hydration and brightness and a modest wrinkle improvement, with very high variation between trials and no severe side effects. 3

  3. Meta-analysis2019People with osteoarthritis in randomized placebo-controlled trials

    Effect of collagen supplementation on osteoarthritis symptoms: a meta-analysis of randomized placebo-controlled trials

    Collagen lowered total WOMAC and VAS scores and stiffness versus placebo, but WOMAC pain and function subscores did not differ. 4

Questions for your clinician

  • Is the evidence for collagen strong enough to justify trying it for my skin or joints, compared with better-studied options?
  • Could any of my allergies or health conditions make a collagen supplement a poor choice?
  • What change would count as a real improvement, and how would we measure it?

Status in the US

Dietary supplement Dietary supplement; not FDA-evaluated for these uses. Collagen peptides are sold as dietary supplements. Under US law, FDA does not approve supplements for safety or effectiveness before they are marketed. 1
