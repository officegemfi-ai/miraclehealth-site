What it is

DSIP (delta sleep-inducing peptide), or emideltide, is a chain of nine amino acids first found in blood draining from rabbits' brains and later made in the lab. FDA found it is not a registered medicine in any country it searched, and it is not FDA-approved. It can cross from the blood into the brain, but enzymes in blood and tissue break it down within minutes.

Limited evidence

Small studies from the 1980s and 1990s, most with under 20 people; two small placebo-controlled trials found little or no sleep benefit, and FDA identified no data supporting effectiveness.

What it may do

  • Limited evidence

    Better sleep continuity reported in small early studies of adults with chronic insomnia, mostly from one research group; the placebo-controlled studies mostly found no clear benefit, and FDA called the evidence inconclusive 6

  • Limited evidence

    Fewer withdrawal signs reported in an uncontrolled study of inpatients withdrawing from alcohol or opioids 6

  • Limited evidence

    Fewer sleep attacks reported in a single case of narcolepsy 6

Risks and side effects

  • Uncommon

    Drop in blood pressure at the first injection, seen in 2 of 107 people in a 1984 withdrawal study 4

  • Uncommon

    Minor effects such as sweating, headache, nausea and dizziness, in 9 of 107 people in the same study 4

  • Not known

    Anxiety and marked insomnia returning after stopping, reported in withdrawal studies 46

  • In theory

    Possible immune reactions to peptide clumps or impurities, which FDA says injection under the skin may increase 4

  • Not known

    No toxicity or addiction-potential studies, and no safety data for injection under the skin 4

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (no safety data)
  • Low blood pressure, or taking medicines that lower blood pressure
  • Going through opioid or alcohol withdrawal (proven, supervised treatments exist)
  • Insomnia or narcolepsy that a clinician has not yet evaluated (approved treatments exist)

Interactions

  • None well documented

Blood markers to watch

Not an approved medicine. These are checks a clinician or a clinical trial would use, not a plan for taking it on your own.

Before starting

  • General health check, including blood pressure

While taking it

None routinely needed.

No evidence-based monitoring exists for DSIP; low blood pressure was reported in one older study.

Key studies

  1. Review2026Published studies in adults with insomnia (6 to 18 people each), one narcolepsy case and two opioid-withdrawal studies

    FDA Evaluation of Emideltide – Related Bulk Drug Substances

    FDA found the studies small and poorly controlled, with two small placebo-controlled insomnia trials showing little benefit, and identified no data supporting effectiveness for insomnia, narcolepsy or opioid withdrawal. 6

  2. Review2026107 people in a 1984 withdrawal study, plus FDA adverse event database search

    Emideltide-related Bulk Drug Substances: Emideltide (Free Base) and Emideltide Acetate

    FDA judged human safety insufficiently characterized: a few people had low blood pressure or minor effects, the FDA database held no reports, and no toxicity studies exist. 4

Questions for your clinician

  • What might be causing my sleep problem, and should I be checked for conditions like sleep apnea?
  • Would an approved sleep treatment or therapy for insomnia suit me better?
  • Is my blood pressure low enough that a further drop could be a problem?

Status in the US

Not approved Not FDA-approved; advisers voted against compounding. FDA dropped emideltide from its Category 2 safety-risk list in April 2026 after the original nomination was withdrawn, which did not make it legal to compound. FDA still reviewed it: in July 2026 its advisers voted 7 to 6 (1 abstention) against adding it to the 503A compounding list, in line with FDA staff advice, and no peptide had been added to that list as of mid-October 2026. 12345
