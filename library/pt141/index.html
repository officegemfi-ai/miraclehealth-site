What it is

PT-141, also called bremelanotide, is a synthetic peptide that activates melanocortin receptors. It is the active ingredient in Vyleesi, an injection FDA approved in 2019 for premenopausal women with low sexual desire that causes distress. Its label says how it improves desire is unknown. Small early studies in men tested it for erections, but it is not approved for men.

Strong evidence

Two 24-week randomized, placebo-controlled trials in about 1,250 premenopausal women supported its approval, though the gains in desire were modest and satisfying sexual events did not increase; in men, only small early-phase studies exist.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Modestly higher sexual desire and less distress about low desire than placebo over 24 weeks in premenopausal women with hypoactive sexual desire disorder 12

  • Limited evidence

    Erections measured by a rigidity monitor more often than with placebo in small early studies of healthy men and men with erectile dysfunction, including men who had not responded well to Viagra; not approved for men 45

Risks and side effects

  • Common

    Nausea in 40% of women in trials (about 1% on placebo); 13% needed anti-nausea medicine and 8% quit because of it 1

  • Common

    Flushing (20%), injection-site reactions (13% vs 8% on placebo) and headache (11%) 1

  • Common

    A brief rise in blood pressure and drop in heart rate after each use, usually gone within 12 hours; the label does not recommend it for people at high risk of heart disease 1

  • Uncommon

    Darkened skin patches on the face, gums or breasts: 1% in the main trials, but 38% in a separate study in which it was given daily, far more often than the label allows; more likely with darker skin, and not always fading 1

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Uncontrolled high blood pressure or known heart or blood vessel disease (contraindicated)
  • High risk of heart disease (not recommended by the label)
  • Pregnancy or trying to conceive (the label advises effective contraception and stopping if pregnancy is suspected)
  • Taking naltrexone for alcohol or opioid addiction
  • Men and postmenopausal women (not approved for them)

Interactions

  • Naltrexone taken by mouth: bremelanotide can sharply lower naltrexone levels; the label advises avoiding it with naltrexone products for alcohol or opioid addiction
  • Oral medicines that need a steady blood level to work, such as some antibiotics: it may slow stomach emptying and reduce their absorption

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

  • Blood pressure check and a review of heart risk

While taking it

None routinely needed.

Blood pressure is the main thing to check, because it rises briefly after each use and the drug is ruled out with uncontrolled hypertension or heart disease; the label's warnings do not involve blood tests.

Key studies

  1. RCT20191,267 premenopausal women with hypoactive sexual desire disorder

    Bremelanotide for the Treatment of Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder: Two Randomized Phase 3 Trials

    In two 24-week trials, desire scores rose and distress scores fell more than with placebo, by modest margins (0.30 to 0.42 points on the desire scale). Nausea, flushing and headache each affected 10% or more of treated women. 2

  2. Label20251,247 women in two 24-week placebo-controlled trials

    VYLEESI (bremelanotide) prescribing information (revised January 2025)

    The number of satisfying sexual events did not increase versus placebo. 18% stopped because of side effects, versus 2% on placebo, most often for nausea. 1

  3. Trial2004Healthy men and men with mild to moderate erectile dysfunction (numbers not given in the abstract)

    Double-blind, placebo-controlled evaluation of the safety, pharmacokinetic properties and pharmacodynamic effects of intranasal PT-141, a melanocortin receptor agonist, in healthy males and patients with mild-to-moderate erectile dysfunction

    A nasal form produced erections, measured by a rigidity monitor, more than placebo at the higher amounts tested. Flushing and nausea were the most common side effects. 4

  4. Trial2004Healthy men and men with an inadequate response to Viagra (numbers not given in the abstract)

    Evaluation of the safety, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamic effects of subcutaneously administered PT-141, a melanocortin receptor agonist, in healthy male subjects and in patients with an inadequate response to Viagra

    Injected PT-141 produced measurable erections compared with placebo in both groups and was reported as well tolerated. 5

Questions for your clinician

  • Could a medicine, mood, relationship or hormone issue be behind my low desire, and should we address that first?
  • Are my blood pressure and heart health safe for a drug that briefly raises blood pressure?
  • If I am a man or past menopause, which approved options fit me better, since this one is not approved for me?

Status in the US

FDA-approved Approved (Vyleesi) for low desire in premenopausal women. Approved in 2019 for acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder in premenopausal women; the label says it is not for postmenopausal women or men and not to enhance sexual performance. Because bremelanotide is an ingredient of an approved drug, federal rules appear to let licensed pharmacies use it in compounded prescriptions (copies of an approved drug are restricted), and any use outside the label is off-label. 13
