What it is

Pramlintide is a synthetic version of amylin, a hormone that the pancreas's beta cells release along with insulin after meals; it differs from human amylin at three amino acid positions. It slows stomach emptying, blunts the rise in glucagon after meals and increases fullness, which lowers after-meal blood sugar and food intake. It acts briefly, with a half-life of about 48 minutes, and was given as an injection under the skin.

Strong evidence

FDA approval as an add-on to mealtime insulin, based on placebo-controlled trials in adults with type 1 and type 2 diabetes; the A1C improvement over placebo was modest, about 0.3 points.

What it may do

  • Strong evidence

    Slightly lower A1C when added to insulin in adults with type 1 diabetes: about 0.25 to 0.34 points more than placebo at 6 months in three trials 1

  • Strong evidence

    Slightly lower A1C when added to insulin in adults with type 2 diabetes: about 0.3 points more than placebo at 6 months in two trials 1

  • Strong evidence

    Modest weight loss instead of the small gain seen with placebo: about 0.8 to 1.6 kg in type 1 and 1.4 to 1.6 kg in type 2 diabetes trials (not approved for weight loss) 12

Risks and side effects

  • Common

    Boxed warning: severe low blood sugar when used with insulin, especially in type 1 diabetes, usually within about 3 hours after an injection; in type 1 trials, 16.8% versus 10.8% on placebo had a severe episode in the first 3 months, and serious injury can follow if it strikes while driving or operating machinery 12

  • Common

    Nausea, loss of appetite and vomiting, mostly early in treatment: nausea affected 48% versus 17% on placebo in type 1 diabetes and 28% versus 12% in type 2 1

  • Common

    Headache: 13% versus 7% on placebo in type 2 diabetes trials 1

Talk to a clinician first if you are

  • Hypoglycemia unawareness (trouble sensing low blood sugar)
  • Confirmed gastroparesis (very slow stomach emptying)
  • Severe low blood sugar needing help in the past 6 months, A1C above 9%, or trouble keeping up with insulin and glucose checks (the label says not to consider it)
  • Children and teens under 18 (not recommended)
  • Past serious allergic reaction to pramlintide

Interactions

  • Insulin: higher risk of severe low blood sugar, and mixing the two in one syringe changes how both act
  • Drugs that slow the gut, such as anticholinergics (for example atropine), and alpha-glucosidase inhibitors (such as acarbose): not studied together and not recommended
  • Oral medicines that need to act quickly, such as pain relievers, antibiotics or birth control pills: slower stomach emptying can delay them; the label advises on timing
  • Other drugs that can raise the risk of low blood sugar, such as ACE inhibitors, fluoxetine, MAO inhibitors, salicylates and sulfonamide antibiotics: use with caution
  • Beta-blockers: can hide the warning signs of low blood sugar

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

  • HbA1c
  • Recent home glucose readings and any history of low blood sugar

While taking it

  • Blood glucose before and after meals and at bedtime, especially when starting or changing treatment
  • HbA1c

The label calls for frequent glucose checks around meals and at bedtime because severe low blood sugar is the main danger, and it treats a rise in milder lows as a warning sign; it lists no other routine blood tests.

Key studies

  1. Label2019Adults with type 1 diabetes using mealtime insulin (placebo-controlled trials)

    SYMLIN (pramlintide acetate) injection, for subcutaneous use: Prescribing Information

    A1C fell 0.25 to 0.34 points more than with placebo at 6 months in three trials (a fourth showed no meaningful difference), with 0.8 to 1.6 kg average weight loss; severe low blood sugar in the first 3 months: 16.8% versus 10.8%. 1

  2. Label2019Adults with type 2 diabetes using insulin (placebo-controlled trials)

    SYMLIN (pramlintide acetate) injection, for subcutaneous use: Prescribing Information

    A1C fell 0.30 to 0.34 points more than with placebo at 6 months; weight fell 1.4 to 1.6 kg versus small gains with placebo; severe low blood sugar in the first 3 months: 8.2% versus 2.1%. 1

Questions for your clinician

  • Is pramlintide still available to me, and if not, what are the alternatives?
  • How should my insulin plan change to lower the risk of severe lows?
  • Is it safe for me to drive in the hours after an injection?

Status in the US

FDA-approved FDA-approved (Symlin); no longer marketed in the US. Approved as an add-on to mealtime insulin for adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes who have not reached their blood sugar goals despite optimal insulin therapy. A drug reference (AHFS, reviewed April 2026) says pramlintide has been discontinued in the US; the date and reason were not found. 12
