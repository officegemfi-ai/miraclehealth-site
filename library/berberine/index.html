What it is

Berberine is an isoquinoline alkaloid found in plants such as goldenseal, barberry and Oregon grape, which have a long history of use in Ayurvedic and Chinese medicine. It is sold as a supplement for blood sugar, cholesterol and weight. In healthy volunteers it slowed several liver enzymes that break down common medicines, which raises concern about drug interactions.

Moderate evidence

Dozens of randomized trials, almost all in China and many poorly reported, show modest blood-sugar lowering in type 2 diabetes; weight-loss studies are fewer and at high risk of bias.

What it may do

  • Moderate evidence

    Lowers blood sugar in adults with type 2 diabetes: HbA1c fell about 0.6 percentage points versus control in the 31 of 37 trials that reported it, often as an add-on to diabetes drugs 34

  • Limited evidence

    Modest drops in weight and BMI in a review of 18 studies, mostly in people with diabetes or fatty liver; many studies had a high risk of bias 1

  • Limited evidence

    May lower cholesterol, and may add to blood pressure lowering when used with amlodipine 2

Risks and side effects

  • Not known

    Digestive upset such as nausea, abdominal pain, bloating, constipation and diarrhea 12

  • Not known

    Drug interactions: it slowed the enzymes CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 in healthy men, which can raise levels of medicines they clear, and it interacts with the transplant drug cyclosporine 157

  • Not known

    Can cause or worsen jaundice in newborns and could lead to kernicterus, a life-threatening buildup of bilirubin that can damage the brain 21

Who should avoid it or check with a clinician first

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding
  • Infants and newborns
  • Taking cyclosporine or other prescription medicines, including diabetes drugs (clinician first)

Interactions

  • Diabetes medicines: NIH advises people who take diabetes drugs to tell their health care providers about herbal supplements, since combining them can cause unwanted effects
  • Cyclosporine (transplant drug): berberine has been shown to interact with it
  • Medicines cleared by CYP2D6, CYP2C9 or CYP3A4, such as dextromethorphan, losartan and midazolam: berberine slowed their breakdown in healthy men
  • Metformin: an extract of goldenseal, a berberine-containing herb, lowered metformin levels in healthy adults

Blood markers to watch

Before starting

  • Fasting glucose and HbA1c if you are using it for blood sugar

While taking it

  • HbA1c or glucose checks to judge the effect, especially alongside diabetes medicines

Blood sugar measures show whether it is working; liver tests are not specifically needed, since LiverTox has found no published cases of liver injury from berberine.

Key studies

  1. Meta-analysis20223,048 adults with type 2 diabetes in 37 randomized trials (36 in China)

    Glucose-lowering effect of berberine on type 2 diabetes: A systematic review and meta-analysis

    Berberine lowered fasting glucose, post-meal glucose and HbA1c (by about 0.6 points) versus control without more side effects or low blood sugar in those trials; many trials reported randomization and blinding poorly. 3

  2. Trial2012Healthy men (randomized crossover)

    Repeated administration of berberine inhibits cytochromes P450 in humans

    Two weeks of berberine slowed drug breakdown by CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4, raising midazolam exposure by about 40%; the authors say drug interactions should be considered. 5

  3. Review2020Published case reports and drug-induced liver injury registries

    Berberine

    Found no published cases of clinically apparent liver injury and rated berberine an unlikely cause (likelihood score E); side effects were mostly mild, short-lived digestive symptoms. 6

Questions for your clinician

  • Could berberine interact with any of my medicines, especially ones broken down by the liver?
  • If I have diabetes, how should we monitor my blood sugar if I add berberine?
  • Is there an approved treatment with stronger evidence for my blood sugar or weight goals?

Status in the US

Dietary supplement Dietary supplement. Sold as a dietary supplement. NIH's complementary health center says there isn't enough rigorous evidence to judge it for weight loss, and that it should not be used during pregnancy or breastfeeding or given to infants. 12
