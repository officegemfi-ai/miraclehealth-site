Low-dose naltrexone (LDN).

Also called LDN, Naltrexone

Naltrexone is an opioid-blocking drug for addiction. In much smaller amounts, people take it for chronic pain and inflammatory conditions, which it isn't approved for. Small trials have given mixed results.

What it isevidence librarian

Naltrexone is a prescription drug that blocks opioid receptors. At its standard strength it is approved to treat alcohol dependence and to block the effects of opioids. Low-dose naltrexone (LDN) uses a small fraction of that amount. That amount is not sold as an approved product, so compounding pharmacies make it. Researchers think low doses may calm immune cells in the brain and spinal cord by blocking a receptor called TLR4, or may briefly raise the body's own endorphins. These ideas are unproven.

Limited evidence

The best trial, in 99 women with fibromyalgia, found no pain benefit over placebo. A review of two small Crohn's disease trials found the evidence too thin for firm conclusions. Other studies are small.

What it may doevidence librarian

  • Strong evidence

    At its standard strength, treats alcohol dependence and blocks the effects of opioids as part of a treatment plan (approved uses, not low-dose) 1

  • Moderate evidence

    No benefit shown for pain: no better than placebo in a 12-week trial of 99 women with fibromyalgia; memory problems improved slightly, a result the authors said may be due to chance 3

  • Limited evidence

    Earlier, much smaller fibromyalgia trials (10 and 30 women) had suggested less pain than with placebo 2

  • Limited evidence

    No clear benefit shown in active Crohn's disease: two small trials (46 people) found no significant difference in remission, though more adults improved on endoscopy; the evidence is low certainty 4

Risks and side effectssafety agent

  • Common

    Vivid dreams: 39% versus 18% on placebo in the fibromyalgia trial 3

  • Common

    Diarrhea, dizziness and hot flashes, each in about 3 in 10 women versus about 1 in 7 on placebo in that trial; headache and nausea were as common as on placebo 3

  • Not known

    Blocks opioid medicines: in people taking or dependent on opioids it can bring on sudden withdrawal, sometimes severe enough to need hospital care, and opioid pain, cough or diarrhea medicines may not work 15

  • Not known

    Opioid overdose (a label warning for standard-strength naltrexone; its relevance at low doses is unknown): after stopping, or when trying to override the block, an opioid dose used before can now be life-threatening, and fatal overdoses have been reported 1

  • Not known

    Liver injury is a label warning for naltrexone; signs of hepatitis need prompt medical review 15

  • Not known

    Long-term safety at low doses is largely unknown 2

Who should skip it, or ask a doctor first

  • Taking opioid pain medicines or other opioids (including tramadol), being dependent on opioids, or on opioid treatment for addiction
  • Liver disease or hepatitis (needs a doctor's review first)
  • Past allergic reaction to naltrexone
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (no adequate studies in pregnancy)
  • Depression or past suicidal thoughts (the naltrexone label calls for monitoring)

What it can clash with

  • Opioids, including pain medicines such as tramadol and opioid-containing cough, cold and diarrhea medicines: their effect is blocked, with a risk of sudden withdrawal
  • Thioridazine: sleepiness and lethargy reported when taken together
  • Disulfiram: the safety of the combination is unknown

Blood tests to watchmarkers agent

Before starting

  • Liver function tests (liver injury is a label warning for naltrexone)

While taking it

None routinely needed.

No monitoring plan has been established for low-dose use; a baseline liver panel is reasonable because the naltrexone label warns about liver injury.

The main studiesevidence librarian

  1. RCT202399 women with fibromyalgia (12 weeks)

    Naltrexone versus placebo in women with fibromyalgia: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial

    Pain fell no more than with placebo (difference 0.34 points on a 0 to 10 scale, not significant). Vivid dreams were more common on naltrexone; the one serious adverse event was in the placebo group. 3

  2. Systematic review20182 trials, 46 people (34 adults, 12 children) with active Crohn's disease

    Low dose naltrexone for induction of remission in Crohn's disease

    Remission in adults was 30% versus 18% on placebo (not significant), while endoscopic response was 72% versus 25%. The evidence was low certainty and too limited for firm conclusions; no serious adverse events were reported. 4

  3. Review2014Small trials in fibromyalgia, Crohn's disease and multiple sclerosis

    The use of low-dose naltrexone (LDN) as a novel anti-inflammatory treatment for chronic pain

    Describes a possible anti-inflammatory action through TLR4 on immune cells of the nervous system; the authors called the clinical data very preliminary and said little is known about long-term safety at low doses. 2

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Would LDN interfere with any opioid or other medicine I might need, including for surgery or an emergency?
  • What does the trial evidence show for my specific condition?
  • How would we judge whether it is helping?

Where it stands in the USsafety agent

Compounded by prescription Off-label; low-dose forms are compounded. Naltrexone tablets are approved to treat alcohol dependence and to block the effects of opioids. No low-dose product is approved or sold commercially: low-dose use for pain or inflammation is off-label, and the low-dose medicine used in studies comes from compounding pharmacies. 123
